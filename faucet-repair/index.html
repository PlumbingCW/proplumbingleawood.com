When faucet trouble is an emergency

A drip is annoying. A fixture that cannot be shut off is different, because water keeps flowing until the supply is closed. If the shutoff valves under the sink are stuck or leak when turned, close the main and call. We handle faucet emergencies at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

A slow drip can also be costly. The EPA's WaterSense program notes that a faucet dripping once per second wastes thousands of gallons a year, so small leaks add up.

What fails inside a faucet

Most faucets fail in a handful of places:

  • Cartridge or ball: worn seals cause drips from the spout.
  • O-rings: leaks around the handle or at the base of a swivel spout.
  • Aerator: clogged by mineral buildup, which lowers flow.
  • Supply lines: braided hoses under the sink that corrode or burst.
  • Shutoff valves: stuck or seeping valves that need replacement.

Many faucet brands use proprietary cartridges. A repair kit often fixes a drip, while an old or discontinued model may cost more to repair than to replace.

Leawood water and your fixtures

Mineral scale builds on aerators and cartridges. WaterOne has published a hardness goal of 120 to 140 mg/L for treated water, an older figure, so look at its current annual report. Soaking an aerator in vinegar clears light scale, and a stuck cartridge often points to buildup.

If every faucet in the house has low pressure, the faucet is not the cause. Look at the water service line or the pressure regulator.

Kitchen, bathroom and outdoor faucets are different jobs

A kitchen faucet works harder than any other in the house. It runs hot water several times a day, carries a sprayer hose and takes the beating of pots and dishes. Its cartridge, sprayer diverter and supply hoses all wear, and a pull-down hose that sticks is a common complaint.

A bathroom faucet is simpler, though its small drain pop-up assembly collects hair and fails often. A leak beneath the sink may be the pop-up rod seal rather than the faucet. Outdoor hose bibs fail in a different way. A hose left on through a freeze can split the pipe behind the wall, and the leak appears when the water is turned on in spring.

For that reason, disconnect hoses before the first hard freeze. If a hose bib drips from the handle, the packing nut often needs a small adjustment, and a frost-free model with a long stem protects the pipe inside the wall.

Signs the shutoff valves need work too

Two small valves under every sink are the emergency brake for the fixture. They are rarely used, so they seize or leak when you finally turn them. If a valve weeps at the stem, drips at the connection or will not turn, replace it as part of the repair. Installing a new quarter-turn valve takes a few minutes and means the next repair is easy.

A braided supply line that is more than ten years old is another candidate. The mesh hides corrosion on the fittings, and a burst line under a sink pours water until someone finds the main. Replace old lines when you replace a faucet.

Flow rates and replacement faucets

Bathroom faucets that carry the EPA WaterSense label are limited to 1.5 gallons per minute. If you replace a bathroom faucet, a lower rating cuts water use without much change in feel. Match the mounting holes and supply connections to the sink so the swap is simple.

What WaterSense guidelines say

According to the EPA WaterSense program, a labeled bathroom faucet flows at 1.5 gallons per minute or less. Keep that standard in mind if a repair turns into a replacement.
