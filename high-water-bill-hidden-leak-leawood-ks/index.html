High Water Bill in Leawood, KS? How to Find a Hidden Leak

A high water bill with no change in habits usually means a leak somewhere. The meter test and the toilet dye test find most of them in about twenty minutes, and neither needs special tools. Here is the order to check.

By the Leawood Pro Plumbing team · Updated November 12, 2026

Why a bill jumps

A bill can rise for a few reasons that are not leaks, such as guests, a new irrigation schedule or a long pool or tub fill. If none of those apply, suspect a leak. The EPA's WaterSense program says household leaks waste nearly 10,000 gallons of water a year in an average home, and about 10 percent of homes lose 90 gallons or more a day.

It also says a family of four that uses more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter probably has leaks. Winter is a useful time to check, since irrigation is off.

The meter test

  • Turn off every faucet and appliance, including the ice maker and the irrigation system.
  • Find your water meter and open the cover.
  • Note the low-flow indicator or the dial reading.
  • Wait fifteen minutes without using water.
  • If the dial or indicator moved, you have a leak.

Next, close the main valve at the house and check the meter again. If it stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is in the line between the meter and the house.

The toilet dye test

Toilets are the most common source of a silent leak, since a bad flapper trickles water into the bowl without a sound. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper leaks. A new flapper is inexpensive, and toilet repair covers the rest.

Other places to look

  • Faucets and showerheads that drip.
  • Water heater relief valve discharge line.
  • Outdoor hose bibs and irrigation valves.
  • Under sinks, at supply lines and shutoff valves.
  • Ceilings below bathrooms and walls near laundry.
  • Soft or green spots in the yard along the service line.

Check the water heater area for dampness, and see water heater repair if a fitting seeps.

When to call for detection

If the meter shows a leak and your checks find nothing, the leak is hidden. A slab leak, a leak in a wall or a buried line cannot be found by looking. Acoustic and thermal methods find it, and our leak detection page explains them.

Seasonal causes that look like leaks

Not every spike is a leak, and a few seasonal causes are worth ruling out. A sprinkler system set for summer can use thousands of gallons a month, and an irrigation valve stuck open can run all night. Filling a pool or a large tub, washing cars and watering new sod each add up. Houseguests and holiday gatherings raise use too.

If your bill rises only in summer, check the irrigation system first. Look for wet spots near heads, valves that do not close, and a controller set to run too often. If the bill rises in winter, when irrigation is off, a leak is more likely.

Documenting the problem

Write down the meter reading at the same time each day for three days. A steady rise with nobody home confirms a leak. Photograph the meter, any wet areas and the repair afterward. If you ask the utility to review your bill, those records support your case, and they help your insurer if damage followed.

The ten minute toilet and fixture check

Toilets cause most hidden household leaks. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait ten minutes without flushing, and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper leaks. A toilet that runs on its own every few minutes does the same thing.

Then check faucets, showerheads, the water heater relief valve and any outdoor hose bibs. A single dripping faucet can waste thousands of gallons over a year, and a leaking flapper can waste far more. These are cheap fixes, and they explain a surprising share of high bills.

When the leak is outside the house

If the meter moves with everything off and the fixtures check out, the leak may be in the line between the meter and the house. Signs include a soggy patch of lawn, an unusually green stripe in the grass, or the sound of water near the meter box. The customer-owned part of that line is the owner's responsibility, so get it located before anyone digs. The water service line page explains repair choices.

How much a leak can cost

The EPA reports that the average household's leaks can waste nearly 10,000 gallons of water a year, and a running toilet can waste much more. That is why a bill that rises with no change in habits deserves a meter test before anything else.

What the EPA says about leaks

According to the EPA, the average household loses nearly 10,000 gallons a year to leaks. The WaterSense guidelines suggest checking toilets and the meter first, and that is the order used above.

Local note: the meter, the bill and the pipe

WaterOne supplies Leawood, and it is the utility to ask about your account and meter readings. It states that the customer-owned section of the service line is the customer's responsibility to repair, replace or maintain. If your meter moves with the house shut off, ask whether the leak is before or after the meter, and use the test above to tell.

Before any digging on a service line, call 811 through Kansas One Call. Residents of Overland Park and Prairie Village are on the same utility, so these steps apply there too.
