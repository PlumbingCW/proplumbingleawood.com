When a commercial plumbing problem is an emergency

For a business, plumbing trouble is lost revenue by the hour. A restroom that overflows, a kitchen floor drain that backs up, a broken line over inventory or a water heater that fails in a salon all call for same-day attention. We handle commercial emergencies at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

Health rules for food service and public restrooms make some failures a closing issue, so we explain what has to be fixed before you reopen.

Restaurants and grease

Kitchen drains carry fats, oils and food waste. A grease interceptor captures some of it, but fat still coats the pipes downstream and eventually blocks them. Hydro jetting on a schedule removes buildup before it fails on a busy night, and a camera inspection shows the condition of the line. See sewer line cleaning for main line blockages.

Offices, retail and multi-tenant buildings

Shared lines mean a problem in one suite can show up in another. Finding the source matters before assigning responsibility. We track down leaks with leak detection and map where each line runs, which also helps landlords and tenants settle who pays.

Responsibility for private service lines follows ownership. Johnson County Wastewater says the property owner maintains the private sewer service line, and WaterOne says the same of the customer-owned section of the water service line. Building owners should know where their section starts.

Planning ahead so a failure does not close the doors

The cheapest emergency is the one that never happens. For a restaurant, that means a written cleaning schedule for floor drains, grease lines and the interceptor. For an office or retail unit, it means knowing where the main shutoff is, testing it each year and posting its location where every manager can find it.

A short list helps whoever is on duty at 9 p.m. Write down the building main valve, the water heater shutoff, the floor drain locations and the 24/7 number, then keep it near the register or the front desk. Staff who know to close one valve can save a stock room.

Walk the property once a quarter. Look for stains on ceiling tiles, damp spots under sinks, running toilets and slow floor drains. Small signs usually show up weeks before a failure, and a quick call about a slow drain is cheaper than a closed kitchen. After a repair, ask for a short written note of what failed and what was done, since landlords, insurers and health inspectors may ask for it.

Tenants, landlords and who calls first

In a multi-tenant building, a plumbing problem raises a practical question: whose pipe is it? Generally, the landlord handles shared lines and the tenant handles fixtures inside the suite, though leases differ. Read the lease before an emergency, and agree who calls the plumber and who pays when a line serves several suites.

When we arrive, we find the source first, so the right party is billed for the right repair. That also keeps a backup in one suite from becoming a dispute with the neighbor.

Grease and pretreatment rules

Restaurants and food service businesses usually need a grease interceptor, and the rules depend on the utility and the city. Johnson County Wastewater runs the sewer collection system, so ask it and the Leawood building department what applies to your space before any change. The International Plumbing Code (IPC) is the model most cities start from.
