When a disposal problem is urgent

A humming, jammed unit is a nuisance. Water leaking from the unit or the pipes under the sink is worse, because it soaks a cabinet and can ruin a floor. A kitchen sink that backs up with a disposal attached also stops cooking and cleanup. We handle disposal emergencies at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

Never reach into a disposal. Turn off power at the switch or breaker, then use the wrench slot on the bottom of many units to free a jam.

What goes wrong with a disposal

  • Hum but no spin: a jam, often a utensil, bone or fruit pit.
  • Nothing at all: tripped reset button or dead switch.
  • Leak at the top: failed sink flange seal.
  • Leak at the bottom: cracked housing, which means replacement.
  • Bad smell: food buildup, which a cleaning solves.
  • Slow drain: a clog in the trap or the kitchen line.

We clear the jam, press the reset and test the unit. If the motor is burned out or the housing leaks, replacement is the fix.

Repair or replace

Most disposals last roughly 8 to 15 years. A unit that is over 10 years old, hums with no spin after clearing a jam, or leaks from the body is usually cheaper to replace than repair. Higher horsepower models handle tougher loads and run quieter.

What not to put in a disposal

Grease, fibrous scraps such as celery and corn husks, coffee grounds in bulk, pasta and rice that swell, and bones all cause clogs. Grease coats pipes and builds up downstream, which can reach the main line. If the kitchen drain keeps clogging, drain cleaning or hydro jetting may be needed.

Installing a new disposal

We install a new unit on a mounting assembly and connect the drain and the dishwasher line. If the sink has no outlet, we add a wiring connection and a switch. A new installation needs an electrical outlet under the sink and a trap arrangement that works. See plumbing installation for related work.

Choosing a replacement disposal

Disposals come in several grades. A basic half horsepower unit with a galvanized grinding chamber suits light use. Three-quarter and one horsepower units grind finer, jam less and run quieter, and stainless steel chambers resist corrosion. Continuous-feed models run while you add scraps, and batch-feed models run only with a stopper in place, which some households prefer for safety.

Think about how you cook. A household that rinses plates and cooks from scratch produces more scraps than one that mostly microwaves. Look at the sound insulation too, since a unit under a sink in an open-plan kitchen is heard across the room.

Keeping a disposal healthy

Run cold water before, during and for a few seconds after grinding so fats stay solid and move through the line. Feed scraps gradually instead of in a heap. Grind a handful of ice cubes occasionally to knock off residue, and cut a lemon or orange peel into the unit for a fresh smell.

Never pour grease down the drain, even with the disposal running. It cools in the pipe downstream and hardens, and that buildup is what eventually blocks a kitchen line. If the sink drains slowly even though the disposal runs fine, the clog is past the unit, and a cable or hydro jetting reaches it.

Safety steps before any disposal work

Never put a hand in a disposal, even if it is off. Switch off the breaker and unplug or disconnect the unit first. A hardwired disposal follows the National Electrical Code (NEC), so leave rewiring to a qualified person.

  • Cut power at the breaker before touching a disposal.
  • Use tongs or pliers to remove jammed items.
  • Keep fingers out of the chamber at all times.
  • Call if the unit leaks near its wiring.
