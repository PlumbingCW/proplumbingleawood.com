On-Demand Water Heater Sizing and Flow Rates in Leawood, KS

An on-demand water heater is chosen by flow rate and temperature rise, not by tank size. Get those two numbers right for your Leawood household and the unit delivers hot water without strain, and get them wrong and it runs cold at the worst moment.

By the Leawood Pro Plumbing team · Updated November 12, 2026

The two numbers that decide the size

Every on-demand unit has a rating in gallons per minute at a certain temperature rise. Temperature rise is the difference between the water coming in and the temperature you want. If incoming water is 50 degrees and you want 120, you need a rise of 70 degrees, and the unit's rated flow at that rise is what counts.

A manufacturer's headline flow rate often assumes a modest rise. In winter, with colder incoming water, the same unit delivers less. That is why flow rate must be matched to your coldest month, not an average.

Typical fixture flow rates

Approximate flow by fixture
FixtureGallons per minute
Bathroom faucet1.0 to 1.5
Kitchen faucet1.5 to 2.0
Showerhead1.5 to 2.5
Dishwasher1.0 to 2.0
Washing machine1.5 to 3.0

Add up the fixtures likely to run at once. Two showers and a dishwasher might total 6 gallons per minute. A unit that can only supply 5 at your winter rise will fall short.

What a cold winter does to the rating

Incoming water temperature falls in cold weather, since water from buried lines and surface sources cools. Because the rise grows, the unit has to work harder for each gallon, and its maximum flow drops. A unit that handles summer easily may run out of margin in January.

If you size on winter numbers, you have a unit that performs all year. Many installers also recommend leaving headroom for a future bathroom or a family change.

Gas, electric, and the pipe in the wall

Gas units deliver the highest flow rates for whole-house use. Electric on-demand units suit point-of-use needs, such as a single bathroom, since a whole-house electric model demands a great deal of power. Check the panel and the gas line before choosing. For the install steps, see water heater installation.

Pipe sizing matters as well. A unit fed by a half-inch line starves in a house that needs three-quarter inch. We check the supply during the survey.

Recirculation and wait time

On-demand units heat quickly, but hot water still has to travel the length of the pipes. In a large house, you wait for the cold water in the line to clear. A recirculation pump or a small buffer tank reduces that wait and saves water. It also adds cost, so weigh it against how often you notice the wait.

Special cases that change the plan

Some homes need a different approach than a single whole-house unit. A home with a bathroom at the far end of a long run may do better with a small point-of-use unit there, which avoids the wait for hot water to arrive.

A house with very high simultaneous demand, such as a large family with several bathrooms, may use two units in a staged setup. An accessory dwelling or a garage workshop may call for a compact electric unit near the fixtures.

Each option has a cost, and the right one depends on how the household uses water. We walk through your daily pattern before recommending equipment.

Checking the supply before you buy

Before buying any on-demand unit, confirm the water pressure and the supply pipe size. A unit has a minimum flow needed to ignite, so a low-flow fixture can fail to trigger it, and a faucet at a very low setting may run cold. Low water pressure also limits flow, which shows up as a unit that never reaches its rating.

A quick pressure reading at a hose bib and a look at the incoming pipe tell us most of what we need. If the pipe is undersized or corroded, the repiping page explains the options.

Standards to check before you buy

Gas on-demand units are covered by ANSI Z21.10.3, and the manual shows the minimum flow, the gas size and the vent rules. Compare those guidelines with your house before choosing a model.

Local note: Leawood water sources and the seasons

WaterOne, which serves Leawood, said in an older publication that its supply comes from the Missouri River, the Kansas River and wells south of the Kansas River. It also says seasonal river conditions can change the taste of the water. Surface sources are colder in winter, so winter inlet temperature is the number to size for. We use a conservative figure for your temperature rise and avoid the flow shortfall that follows.

Ask the installer what rise they used. A claim of a "whole-house" unit with no rise stated is not enough information. If you live in Overland Park or Mission Hills, the same sizing method applies, since WaterOne serves both.
